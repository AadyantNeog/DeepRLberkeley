## Part I - From supervised learning to imitation learning

## 1. From supervised prediction to sequential control

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1-429

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

After a brief opening about the early hour and lack of coffee, the lecturer introduced supervised learning of behaviors, also called imitation learning. He began with dashboard-camera images. In an ordinary supervised problem, an input $x$ might be mapped to a class or bounding box $y$ by a neural network with parameters $\theta$. The network represents a conditional distribution over labels, written $p_\theta(y\mid x)$, and training chooses parameters that maximize the summed log likelihood of the ground-truth labels. He identified this familiar objective with maximum-likelihood estimation or empirical risk minimization.

To turn the prediction problem into control, the output becomes a steering-and-throttle command. These may be continuous numbers or discretized into categorical actions. The notation then changes without yet changing the learning problem: the image $x$ becomes observation $o$, the output $y$ becomes action $a$, and $p_\theta$ becomes policy $\pi_\theta$.

Control takes place through time. At each discrete time step the system receives an observation, chooses an action, and later receives another observation. Continuous time could be used, but an implementation would ordinarily discretize it anyway. Adding the subscript $t$ initially looks cosmetic, but it will matter because an action changes the distribution of later observations. That dependence is temporarily set aside while the decision problem is defined.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 3-5 display the progression

$$
p_\theta(y\mid x) \longrightarrow p_\theta(a\mid o) \longrightarrow \pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t),
$$

and slide 3 gives the supervised maximum-likelihood objective

$$
\arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}).
$$

### Additional explanation

The crucial change is causal rather than architectural. In a classifier, an output does not alter the next test input. In control, $a_t$ affects the system and therefore which $o_{t+1}$ the model will see. The same supervised loss can be used, but its deployment distribution is generated by the learned model itself.

## 2. State and observation

**Transcript coverage:** lines 430-792

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer distinguished the observation supplied to the policy from the underlying state of the world. In the highway example, the policy receives camera images, while a task-appropriate state could contain vehicle positions, orientations, and velocities. It need not include every physical detail in the universe: a useful state is chosen at the granularity needed for the decision problem.

The state is denoted $s$. Some algorithms need an explicit state representation, but the imitation-learning methods in this lecture do not need to know, represent, or infer it. Students nevertheless need the concept because later reinforcement-learning methods reason about states.

In Q&A, the lecturer explained why one still uses observations when a state is well defined: observations are what the deployed policy can actually measure. One could train a separate state estimator, but acting directly on camera input is convenient. A policy that maps observations to actions does not necessarily reconstruct the state internally. Simple control rules can ignore irrelevant state components; for example, merely driving straight may not require encoding nearby cars to the left and right. Whether a learned representation becomes a sufficient state representation is a deeper research question to be revisited near the end of the course.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 5 draws $s_t$ as the underlying configuration that produces $o_t$, while the learned policy uses $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)$. This visually confirms that the policy can be observation-based even when the environment is described by a latent state.

### Additional explanation

An observation is sensor data; a state is a predictive abstraction. They coincide only in a fully observed model. A representation inside a neural network can be effective for action prediction without being a complete state, because task performance may require less information than reconstructing all future-relevant variables.

## 3. The dynamical graphical model and conditional independence

**Transcript coverage:** lines 793-1323

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer drew what the transcript repeatedly renders as a “baset”: a dynamic probabilistic graph connecting states, observations, and actions across time. Arrows correspond to conditional probability distributions. Three recurring distributions appear:

- the learned policy, $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)$;
- the observation model, $p(o_t\mid s_t)$, which includes sensor noise and other nuisances; and
- the transition model, $p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$, which describes the system dynamics.

The graph also needs an initial-state prior $p(s_1)$. The imitation-learning algorithm itself does not require the observation or transition probabilities, but later homeworks and algorithms will depend on understanding this structure.

The graph exposes conditional independences. The transcript calls the key property the “Markoff property”: once the current state is known, a future state is conditionally independent of earlier states. Current vehicle positions and velocities should therefore contain whatever past information is needed to predict what happens next.

The same property does not generally hold for observations because a current observation need not reveal the full state. A student asked why actions are conditioned on observations if conditioning on state would be better. The lecturer agreed that a state-based policy could be better, but the state is usually unavailable. A broken camera illustrates the limitation: an observation with no state information cannot support good driving, so an observation-only policy is not generally optimal.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 6-7 visually identify the object as a dynamic Bayesian network and spell the property “Markov.” Slide 6 states

$$
s_{t+1}\perp s_{t-1}\mid s_t.
$$

The transcript’s “baset” and “Markoff” are transcription errors; the corrected terms are confined to this reconciliation layer.

### Additional explanation

The Markov property is a modeling criterion, not a claim that the physical world forgets its history. It says that the chosen state representation already summarizes the relevant history. Observations can be non-Markov even when the latent state is Markov.

In controlled dynamics the precise condition includes the chosen action:

$$
p(s_{t+1},r_t\mid s_{1:t},a_{1:t})
=p(s_{t+1},r_t\mid s_t,a_t).
$$

The slide's state-only independence follows after fixing a memoryless policy and integrating its actions. It need not hold under an arbitrary history-dependent controller unless the controller's memory is included in the state.

## 4. Full observability and valid state design

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1324-1686

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The partially observed formulation contains a policy, observation probability, and transition probability. Most imitation-learning algorithms and many RL algorithms only assume those environment distributions exist; they do not need to know them. Model-based RL is the major exception because it explicitly learns a model before using it for decision making.

A special case is full observability, where $o=s$. The observation nodes can then be removed and the policy written as $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$. Some algorithms require this assumption and others do not. The lecturer warned that he may switch notation from observations to states and would try to say whether the distinction matters.

Asked what makes a valid minimal state, he answered that a valid state must satisfy the property given by the graphical model: the next state is conditionally independent of earlier states given the current state. Validity alone does not make the representation useful. In stock trading, current price alone can define a transition distribution, but may contain too little information for good decisions. Choosing an informative state remains a domain-dependent modeling problem.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 7 contrasts the partially observed case, with $p(o_t\mid s_t)$, against the fully observed case $o_t=s_t$. It also notes that one typically does not need to know the observation and transition distributions for the model-free methods under discussion.

### Additional explanation

There are two separate tests for a state representation:

1. **Predictive sufficiency:** does it make the dynamics Markov?
2. **Decision usefulness:** does it preserve information needed to choose a high-quality action?

A conditional distribution can be fitted to almost any coarse observation, but its existence does **not** prove the Markov property. For example, current price alone may leave history informative about the next price. A decision-sufficient state must preserve reward information as well as transition information; a constant representation can evolve trivially while discarding everything needed to choose rewarding actions.

## 5. Demonstration trajectories and behavioral cloning

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1687-1881

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Returning to partially observed driving, the lecturer proposed solving control with supervised learning. A capable driver produces demonstration trajectories containing synchronized observations and actions. In the example, a camera image plus steering and throttle are saved every tenth of a second, giving one pair every 100 milliseconds. Many such sequences are collected.

Behavioral cloning then maximizes the log probability of each demonstrated action conditioned on the observation from the same time step. Superscript $i$ indexes trajectories and subscript $t$ indexes time within a trajectory. This objective is the core definition students need for Homework 1.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 8 writes the dataset and objective as

$$
\mathcal D=\left\{\left(o_1^{(i)},a_1^{(i)},\ldots,o_H^{(i)},a_H^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
$$

$$
\arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{H}
\log \pi_\theta\!\left(a_t^{(i)}\mid o_t^{(i)}\right).
$$

### Additional explanation

Optimization treats all observation-action pairs much like supervised examples, even though they originated in trajectories. The danger appears later: training samples come from the demonstrator’s visitation distribution, whereas deployment samples come from the learned policy’s visitation distribution.

## 6. ALVINN: an early end-to-end driving system

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1882-1986

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Behavioral cloning is old. The lecturer showed a 1989 autonomous-driving system that today would be described as end-to-end deep learning. The network had only five hidden units, yet under favorable conditions it could drive from black-and-white camera observations. Modern end-to-end driving systems inherit aspects of this design. In response to a question, he clarified that the input image shown in the old paper was a hand-drawn cartoon, apparently because an actual photograph was not included.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 10 names the system **ALVINN: Autonomous Land Vehicle In a Neural Network** and confirms the year 1989, a $30\times32$ video input retina, five hidden units, and 30 output units.

### Additional explanation

ALVINN is historically important because it shows that direct perception-to-control learning predates modern large networks. Its limited operating envelope also foreshadows the central question of this lecture: success on familiar inputs does not guarantee robust behavior after the policy drifts away from familiar trajectories.

## 7. The behavioral-cloning model and action distributions

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1987-2538

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The algorithm has two steps: collect good demonstrations, then apply supervised maximum-likelihood training. Demonstration collection has substantial domain-specific nuance, but the lecture assumes plentiful, well-collected data and focuses on learning.

An image observation is processed by an encoder. The lecturer named contemporary vision transformers, earlier ResNets and convolutional networks, and the tiny one-layer, five-unit MLP used in 1989. The model outputs parameters of an action distribution, from which an action is sampled at test time.

For discrete actions, including Atari key presses or language-model tokens, the model outputs one logit per action. A softmax converts logits into a categorical distribution; standard cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood objective.

For continuous actions, the network can parameterize a multivariate normal distribution with a mean and either diagonal or full covariance. Directly outputting an action and using mean-squared error corresponds to a normal distribution with fixed identity covariance. More expressive choices include diffusion, flow matching, beta distributions for bounded actions, or any differentiable density with learnable parameters.

Diagonal covariance requires only linearly many outputs in the action dimension, while a dense covariance requires quadratically many. Diagonal forms also avoid matrix-invertibility and conditioning problems. Asked whether the final mapping is a decoder, the lecturer said the image-processing portion is normally called an encoder; the last linear layer could be called a decoder, but that term is unnecessarily elaborate for such a simple output.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 12 gives the discrete softmax

$$
p(a_t=j\mid o_t)=\frac{\exp(f_j(o_t))}{\sum_{i=1}^{A}\exp(f_i(o_t))},
$$

and the continuous Gaussian

$$
p(a_t\mid o_t)=\mathcal N\!\left(a_t\mid \mu(o_t),\Sigma(o_t)\right).
$$

For the special case $\Sigma(o_t)=I$, the slide writes the log-likelihood as proportional to the negative squared error; its displayed shorthand omits constants and the minus sign in a way that would be misleading if read as an exact equality. The transcript’s repeated “coariance” transcription variants are corrected here using the slide notation.

### Additional explanation

The output family determines what behaviors the policy can represent. A single Gaussian is convenient but cannot faithfully represent several distinct valid actions. Fixed-variance regression can therefore average incompatible demonstrations, while richer distributions can retain multiple modes.

For fixed identity covariance,

$$
\log\pi_\theta(a\mid o)=-\tfrac12\|a-\mu_\theta(o)\|^2-\tfrac d2\log(2\pi).
$$

Thus maximizing likelihood is equivalent to minimizing squared error. Learned covariance also contributes a log-determinant penalty and must be positive definite; a diagonal model still needs positive variances, usually enforced through a log-scale parameterization. Flow matching and diffusion usually use their own tractable training losses, not this direct Gaussian likelihood.

## Part II - Why naive behavioral cloning can fail

## 8. Why small imitation errors can compound

**Transcript coverage:** lines 2539-2895

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The mechanics of behavioral cloning are simple; the mystery is whether it works. Students suggested several relevant conditions: performance depends on whether training data cover deployment situations, pretrained models may provide useful prior knowledge, recovery examples can teach correction, and failed demonstrations can be copied as failures.

The fundamental theoretical problem is compounding error. Suppose the learned trajectory begins close to a demonstrated trajectory but makes a tiny error. The next state is then slightly different from the data, which can cause a larger error. Repetition can move the policy progressively farther from its training distribution until a driving policy crashes.

Unlike i.i.d. supervised examples, sequential observations are affected by previous actions. A mistake changes the next input, usually in an unfavorable direction, so errors can build on one another.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 14 overlays a black training trajectory with a red expected trajectory from $\pi_\theta$, showing increasing divergence through time. The transcript says the current observation is influenced by “what happened next”; in context and in the slide’s temporal direction, the intended claim is that it is influenced by what happened previously.

### Additional explanation

This is a feedback loop:

$$
\text{prediction error}\rightarrow\text{new state}\rightarrow\text{less familiar input}
\rightarrow\text{larger prediction error}.
$$

Even a low average supervised error can be dangerous when the rare mistake sends the system into a region where no supervised guarantee applies.

## 9. Practical mitigation through recovery data

**Transcript coverage:** lines 2896-3375

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer contrasted the theoretical warning with an NVIDIA driving system from roughly ten years earlier. More data helped: after about 3,000 miles the car drove plausibly, though the demonstration still looked risky.

The particularly important trick was to mount three cameras facing left, center, and right. Images from the right-facing camera were paired with actions biased left; images from the left-facing camera were paired with actions biased right. At test time only the center camera was used. This “hack” created synthetic corrective examples, so a car drifting from lane center learned to steer back.

The method did not fix every possible compounding error, only common lateral deviations. Similar recovery data could be collected by asking a driver to swerve and correct. Modern practice can instead use thoughtful demonstration collection and data augmentation. Behavioral cloning is not generally guaranteed, but careful models, data, recoveries, multitask learning, or a different algorithm can make it useful.

The lecturer summarized the theoretical contrast as follows: ordinary supervised-learning error improves with data under its i.i.d. assumptions, often with a $1/\sqrt N$-type rate, whereas behavioral cloning has worse worst-case scaling because those assumptions fail.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 15-18 credit Bojarski et al. (2016) for the NVIDIA example and diagram the recorded steering-angle adjustment applied to left, center, and right camera images. Slide 17 groups the remedies as changing the algorithm, using a very accurate model, collecting or augmenting data intelligently, and multitask learning.

### Additional explanation

The three-camera construction approximates data the car would see after a small deviation without deliberately placing the deployed car in danger. It works when the likely perturbations and their corrective labels are known, but it is not a universal defense against arbitrary off-distribution states.

A $1/\sqrt N$ rate typically describes an estimation/generalization term under specified capacity and sampling assumptions, not guaranteed decay of total prediction error to zero. Correlated frames also need not provide $N$ independent examples.

## 10. Distributional shift in behavioral cloning

**Transcript coverage:** lines 3376-3975

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Distributional shift means that the training-input distribution differs from the test-input distribution. A model trained on mathematics and tested on ancient Greek literature, or trained on U.S. exit signs and tested on unrelated signs in China, has no general performance guarantee.

For behavioral cloning, the action conditional is not the central mismatch. The policy is trained on observations drawn from the demonstration distribution, denoted $p_{\mathrm{data}}(o)$, but tested on observations generated by running the learned policy, denoted in the transcript as the policy’s observation distribution. Past mistakes make these distributions differ.

A student suggested collecting enough data to cover everything. The lecturer said worst-case coverage can scale very unfavorably because narrow bottlenecks may require exactly the right action, though domain knowledge and careful instructions can make broad practical coverage possible.

The two-lane-road example illustrated a dangerous off-distribution rule. A policy trained only in the correct lane may learn to remain in whichever lane it occupies. After a rare deviation into the oncoming lane, it may competently center itself in exactly the wrong lane. The lecturer then paused for a five-minute intermission, planning to resume at 9:01. During the break, an architecture student working on robotic fabrication asked about enrollment from position 29 on the waitlist; the instructor said he would think about it. Another brief exchange is incomplete in the transcript and cannot be reconstructed reliably.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 19-20 formalize the mismatch as

$$
p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\ne p_{\mathrm{test}}(x),
$$

and, for behavioral cloning,

$$
p_{\mathrm{data}}(o_t)\ne p_{\pi_\theta}(o_t).
$$

The slide uses $p_{\pi_\theta}(o_t)$ for the visitation distribution, avoiding the transcript’s ambiguous wording “$\pi_\theta$ of $o$.”

### Additional explanation

This is often called **covariate shift induced by the policy**. The key complication is that the test distribution is not externally fixed: changing $\theta$ changes both the action rule and the observations on which it will be evaluated.

## Part III - Quantifying compounding error

## 11. What theory can and cannot tell us

**Transcript coverage:** lines 3976-4173

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer asked whether behavioral cloning is worse than supervised learning, whether enough data makes the issue disappear, and whether some control problems amplify it more than others. Theory can reveal how error trades off against quantities such as data and horizon and can describe a worst case. It usually does not prove that a practical system will or will not work, because real problems do not realize every mathematically permitted worst case. Nor does worst-case theory identify typical behavior, which depends strongly on the domain.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 24 explicitly separates “intuition for tradeoffs” and “how bad can it be” from a practical proof or a characterization of typical behavior.

### Additional explanation

A worst-case bound is diagnostic. It identifies which assumptions leave a failure mode possible and which quantities can amplify it. It should not be read as a prediction that every learned controller will attain the bound.

## 12. Mistake cost, state distributions, and horizon

**Transcript coverage:** lines 4174-4513

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

For the analysis, demonstrations come from a good deterministic policy $\pi^*$. The setting is fully observed. A cost of zero is assigned when the learned policy takes the same action as $\pi^*$ and one otherwise. Thus expected cost at one state is the probability of a mistake.

At each time $t$, the learned policy induces a distribution over states that depends on all prior actions. Summing expected cost over those time-indexed state and action distributions gives the expected total number of mistakes. The trajectory length is called the horizon $H$.

When $H=1$, there is no next input on which an error can compound, so behavioral cloning reduces to ordinary supervised prediction. The question is how the mistake count changes as the horizon grows.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 25-26 define

$$
c(s_t,a_t)=
\begin{cases}
0,&a_t=\pi^*(s_t),\\
1,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
$$

and the expected total mistake count

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{H}
\mathbb E_{s_t\sim p_{\pi_\theta}(s_t),\,a_t\sim\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}
[c(s_t,a_t)].
$$

### Additional explanation

The cost is deliberately simple: it compares imitation accuracy rather than task reward. This isolates the distribution-shift mechanism. A later RL objective may care that some deviations are harmless and others catastrophic.

This zero-one loss is appropriate for discrete actions and a deterministic expert. For a continuous stochastic policy, exact equality to one demonstrated real vector usually has probability zero; use an appropriate continuous loss or divergence and a corresponding stability analysis.

## 13. Tightrope worst case: quadratic error

**Transcript coverage:** lines 4514-4914

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Assume supervised learning works on training states: the probability of disagreeing with $\pi^*$ is at most $\epsilon$. The lecturer then constructed a tightrope grid world. Training states lie along one safe row and the correct action is always right. A single up or down action enters an unseen region where future mistakes are treated as virtually certain.

At the first step, a mistake occurs with probability $\epsilon$ and can cause roughly $H$ mistakes. If the first step succeeds, a mistake at the second step has probability approximately $\epsilon$ and causes roughly $H-1$ mistakes, and so forth. There are $H$ terms, each of order $\epsilon H$, so the expected total is $O(\epsilon H^2)$.

This is disappointing because error grows quadratically rather than linearly with task length. It is also deliberately idealized: real neural networks can generalize away from exact training points. The example identifies the difficult structure - one error moves the agent into a region from which it cannot recover and where the supervised guarantee no longer applies.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 27 states the in-distribution assumption

$$
\pi_\theta(a\ne\pi^*(s)\mid s)\le\epsilon,
\qquad s\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{train}},
$$

and concludes

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{H}\mathbb E[c(s_t,a_t)]\le \epsilon H\times H=O(\epsilon H^2).
$$

### Additional explanation

The bound counts the duration of damage as well as the chance of initiating it. A mistake probability of order $\epsilon$ at each of $H$ opportunities, multiplied by up to $H$ downstream affected steps, yields the quadratic product.

## 14. General analysis with total variation

**Transcript coverage:** lines 4915-5976

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The more general assumption bounds expected disagreement under the full training distribution, not just on memorized training points:

$$
\mathbb E_{s\sim p_{\mathrm{train}}}
[\pi_\theta(a\ne\pi^*(s)\mid s)]\le\epsilon.
$$

At time $t$, the policy’s state distribution can be decomposed into a no-prior-mistake component and an unconstrained component. With probability $(1-\epsilon)^t$, no mistake has occurred and the state remains distributed as training data; the remaining mass is called $p_{\mathrm{mistake}}$. The latter need not be bad, but no favorable assumption is available for it.

The lecturer introduced total variation divergence as half the summed absolute difference between two probability mass functions. It ranges from zero to one. Substituting the mixture decomposition into the distance between the learned visitation distribution and training distribution, bounding the unknown component by its worst possible divergence of one, and using $(1-\epsilon)^t\ge 1-\epsilon t$ gives

$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p_{\mathrm{train}},p_{\pi_\theta,t})\le\epsilon t.
$$

In Q&A, he said total variation is useful here because it is simple and can later be related to KL divergence. The linearized bound becomes coarse for enormous $t$; it is intended as a rough scale, with $\epsilon$ interpreted as in-distribution generalization error.

To bound total mistakes, the state expectation is expanded as a sum and $p_{\mathrm{train}}$ is added and subtracted. One term is the in-distribution error, at most $\epsilon$. The other is bounded by worst-case per-step cost one times twice the total variation, at most $2\epsilon t$. Summing $\epsilon+2\epsilon t$ through the horizon again gives $O(\epsilon H^2)$. Thus quadratic scaling survives the more realistic in-distribution generalization assumption.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 28-31 display the following heuristic decomposition, which is not generally exact under the stated assumption (see the corrected proof below)

$$
p_{\pi_\theta}(s_t)=(1-\epsilon)^t p_{\mathrm{train}}(s_t)
+\left(1-(1-\epsilon)^t\right)p_{\mathrm{mistake}}(s_t),
$$

the definition

$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac12\sum_x|p(x)-q(x)|,
$$

and the per-step bound $\epsilon+2\epsilon t$. The slide cites Ross et al., *A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning*, for further analysis.

### Additional explanation

The proof separates what supervised learning controls from what distribution shift leaves uncontrolled. Total variation is useful because for a bounded cost it directly limits how much an expectation can change between two distributions.

**Corrected coupling proof.** The displayed mixture in the lecture is a heuristic, not an identity implied by average expert-distribution error. Write $d_t^*$ for the expert's state distribution at time $t$ and assume $\mathbb E_{s\sim d_t^*}[e(s)]\le\epsilon$ at every time, where $e(s)=1-\pi_\theta(\pi^*(s)\mid s)$. Start expert and learner in the same sampled state and use shared transition randomness while their actions agree. Continue the expert rollout after a disagreement.

The probability that the *first* disagreement is at step $k$ is at most $\mathbb E_{d_k^*}[e(s)]$: the first-disagreement event is a subset of the event that a learner action sampled at the expert's step-$k$ state disagrees. A union bound over the $t-1$ actions before $s_t$ therefore gives

$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_t^*,d_t^\pi)
\le \Pr(\text{some disagreement before }t)
\le \min\{1,(t-1)\epsilon\}.
$$

Conditioning on no disagreement can change the distribution of expert states: states where the learner is accurate are overrepresented among surviving trajectories. Thus the conditional no-error distribution cannot generally be replaced by the unconditional $d_t^*$. Nor does an average error bound imply independent constant-rate errors or survival probability $(1-\epsilon)^t$. A *uniform conditional* error bound gives survival **at least** $(1-\epsilon)^{t-1}$, but still does not justify that mixture identity.

For $f(s)=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[c(s,a)]\in[0,1]$, the sharper bounded-expectation inequality is $|\mathbb E_p f-\mathbb E_q f|\le D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)$ (the lecture's $2D_{\mathrm{TV}}$ is a valid looser bound). Hence

$$
J_{\mathrm{imit}}(\pi)
\le \sum_{t=1}^H\min\{1,t\epsilon\}
\le \min\left\{H,\frac{\epsilon H(H+1)}2\right\}.
$$

This proves the intended $O(\epsilon H^2)$ upper bound without the invalid mixture. Quadratic growth describes the small-error regime; the count can never exceed $H$. These are population assumptions, not guarantees obtained merely from a low training-set loss.

## 15. Why the bound is pessimistic and how recovery helps

**Transcript coverage:** lines 5977-6387

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The quadratic result is a worst case. Real behavior can be better when $H$ is short, $\epsilon$ is extremely small, the model generalizes beyond the training distribution, or the system can recover from errors. The tightrope is severe precisely because every error is irreversible.

Recovery examples can move behavior back toward the training distribution. This produces an apparent paradox: slightly imperfect demonstrations containing intentional mistakes and recoveries may train a more robust policy than flawless narrow demonstrations. Effective behavioral cloning therefore seeks a very small in-distribution error, useful off-distribution generalization, and data that show recovery.

Asked whether recovery can be formalized, the lecturer said one must assume structure in the dynamics. Continuous-control analyses may assume stability; discrete systems may assume appropriate cycles in the transition graph. Without such domain-dependent assumptions, an irreversible tree-like environment remains possible.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 32 explicitly notes that real systems can often recover and warns that imitation learning still requires data demonstrating how. It highlights the same paradox: data with more mistakes and recoveries can lead to better imitation.

### Additional explanation

Stability changes the damage multiplier. If deviations contract back toward familiar states, one mistake need not contaminate every remaining time step, so the effective dependence on horizon can be much milder than $H^2$.

## Part IV - Dataset aggregation

## 16. DAgger: changing the training distribution

**Transcript coverage:** lines 6388-6678

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Rather than demanding a perfect policy whose visitation distribution matches the demonstrations, dataset aggregation changes the data distribution. The lecturer attributed the method to a 2011 paper by Stéphane Ross.

DAgger begins with human demonstrations and trains a policy. That imperfect policy is then run to collect the observations it actually visits. Its actions are discarded; a demonstrator supplies the action that should have been taken at each collected observation. The newly labeled pairs are added to the existing dataset and the policy is retrained. Repeating this process makes policy-generated observations an increasing fraction of the data, reducing the distribution mismatch. In many problems, even one aggregation iteration produces a large improvement.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 35-36 name the algorithm **DAgger: Dataset Aggregation** and list the loop:

1. train $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)$ on human data $\mathcal D$;
2. run it to obtain policy observations $\mathcal D_\pi$;
3. ask the expert to label those observations with actions;
4. update $\mathcal D\leftarrow\mathcal D\cup\mathcal D_\pi$.

### Additional explanation

DAgger is interactive imitation learning. Its guarantee comes from supervising the states the learner visits, not from changing the supervised loss. The new authority it requires is an expert or oracle that can label learner-induced states.

**What DAgger guarantees.** Expert labels alone are insufficient: the policy class must fit them adequately, and the supervised updates must satisfy the online-learning/no-regret assumptions. The analysis controls average learner-distribution loss over iterations and supports a suitable mixture or selected iterate; it does not automatically guarantee the final neural-network checkpoint or convergence of visitation distributions. If the deployed policy's average disagreement on its *own* states is $\epsilon$, its expected disagreement count is $H\epsilon$. Translating disagreement into excess task cost additionally needs a bound on the expert's cost-to-go sensitivity to a wrong action, often related to recoverability. See the [original DAgger analysis](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/ross11a.html).

## 17. DAgger labeling assumptions and implementation Q&A

**Transcript coverage:** lines 6679-7617

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

A forest-drone example exposed DAgger’s practical bottleneck. The drone moves forward at constant speed and its action is left or right, but an expert must label isolated observations without receiving the feedback that would follow the chosen action. This can be manageable in chess, where a player can evaluate a board, but extremely difficult for an agile humanoid mid-backflip.

If an expert can provide a perfect action for any visited observation, DAgger addresses the theoretical problem: the formerly unconstrained $p_{\mathrm{mistake}}$ portion also receives low-error supervision. Whether a person can actually supply such Markovian labels is a separate human-factors question.

The lecturer answered several further questions:

- If a corrected action would lead to an unseen next state, subsequent DAgger iterations encounter and label those new states. A worst case may require many iterations, but repeated labeling eventually repairs the chain.
- The optimal policy need not be unique. The analysis can be generalized to stochastic mixtures by replacing exact action disagreement with a divergence, though the simple cost definition assumed one deterministic policy.
- He did not know a worst-case iteration bound as a function of horizon; empirically DAgger often needs few iterations.
- Dataset growth is usually not the main concern because a large initial demonstration set is followed by a comparatively small amount of policy data used to patch holes.
- Simulation can supply an oracle from privileged state information unavailable at deployment. Mid-2010s board-game and Atari work used Monte Carlo search to produce actions and distill them into a model.
- The policy rollout itself evaluates current performance, so collection can stop when it is good enough.
- All newly labeled examples should generally be retained, including states where the policy was already correct, so retraining does not forget them.
- Worst-case dependence on state-space size is difficult to avoid. Naively discretizing a continuous space yields exponential growth with dimension.
- The DAgger theorem does not care whether retraining is cold-started or warm-started as long as the required in-distribution error is achieved. In practice, one may revert to pretrained weights and fine-tune again; restarting from the pretrained model can help new aggregated labels change earlier behavior.
- Original DAgger asks the expert to label every visited observation rather than letting the expert choose only interesting ones.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 36 credits Ross et al. (2011) for the forest example. Slide 37 visually emphasizes the difficult expert-labeling step. The slide spells the algorithm “DAgger”; transcript variants such as “Dagger” and “DIY” refer to the same method.

### Additional explanation

The oracle assumption is the price of the guarantee. DAgger is strongest when the expert can answer counterfactual queries for arbitrary learner states. When labels depend on hidden history, bodily feedback, or an unrecoverable trajectory, that assumption can fail even if demonstrations are easy to perform normally.

## 18. Intervention-based DAgger

**Transcript coverage:** lines 7618-8430

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

A common practical variant lets the policy act while a human safety operator watches. When the operator dislikes the behavior, the human takes control and the system stores observation-action pairs from the intervention. Unlike post hoc labeling, the operator’s actions affect the system and provide normal feedback.

The lecturer showed a robot fitting a timing belt onto gears. Autonomous segments and expert interventions together succeeded and generated corrective data; that particular system also used some RL and was not pure imitation learning. A box-folding example showed similarly messy, non-optimal interventions used mainly to rescue the robot.

People conventionally choose when to intervene because they can combine broad knowledge of the robot, task, and safety context. In safety-critical driving, the safety driver rather than the car should decide when to take over. Such data emphasize recoveries after something has already gone wrong, not necessarily actions that would have prevented the error.

This is not automatically equivalent to DAgger. If a human never intervenes, distribution shift remains. Choosing an intervention time uniformly at random would label one random learner-visited step per trajectory and eventually reproduce DAgger’s statistical coverage, though inefficiently. Real operators are biased toward bad states and rarely label ordinary correct behavior. Adaptive rules based on predicted failure are possible, but a watching human often makes better practical choices.

Whether labels should come from one consistent expert or many diverse experts depends on the task: precise continuous control favors consistency, while broad generalization favors diversity. Allowing control to switch back to the policy creates a visitation distribution that mixes human and learned policies, making analysis less clean, although this is common in practice.

### Additional explanation

Intervention changes both which states are labeled and which states are visited after takeover. It is safer and more natural for a human, but its sampling bias must be considered. A useful implementation records policy confidence, intervention timing, and who controlled each step so the mixed data can be analyzed rather than treated as homogeneous demonstrations.

## Consolidated takeaways

1. Behavioral cloning uses maximum likelihood on demonstration observation-action pairs.
2. State and observation are distinct; a Markov state summarizes future-relevant history, while an observation may reveal only part of it.
3. A learned policy changes the distribution of its own later inputs.
4. Small imitation errors can compound by moving the policy away from demonstrated trajectories.
5. The relevant shift is from $p_{\mathrm{data}}(o_t)$ to the learner’s visitation distribution $p_{\pi_\theta}(o_t)$.
6. Under minimal assumptions, expected imitation mistakes can grow as $O(\epsilon H^2)$.
7. The quadratic result is a worst case; strong generalization, short horizons, small $\epsilon$, stability, and recovery data can make practice much better.
8. Recovery examples may be more valuable than perfectly narrow demonstrations.
9. DAgger labels states visited by the learner and aggregates them into the training set.
10. DAgger’s theoretical benefit depends on an expert or oracle that can label arbitrary learner-induced observations.
11. Human intervention is a practical, safety-friendly variant but samples states differently from original DAgger.

## Key equations

### Behavioral-cloning objective

$$
\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{H}
\log \pi_\theta\!\left(a_t^{(i)}\mid o_t^{(i)}\right).
$$

### Discrete action policy

$$
\pi_\theta(a_t=j\mid o_t)=
\frac{\exp(f_j(o_t))}{\sum_{i=1}^{A}\exp(f_i(o_t))}.
$$

### Gaussian continuous action policy

$$
\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)=
\mathcal N\!\left(a_t\mid\mu_\theta(o_t),\Sigma_\theta(o_t)\right).
$$

### Expected imitation cost

$$
J_{\mathrm{imit}}(\pi_\theta)=
\sum_{t=1}^{H}
\mathbb E_{s_t\sim p_{\pi_\theta,t},\,a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}
[c(s_t,a_t)].
$$

### Valid coupling bound

$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_t^*,d_t^\pi)\le\min\{1,(t-1)\epsilon\}.
$$

Here $t=1$ is the shared initial state; the average expert-distribution error is at most $\epsilon$ at each time. An exact unconditional no-error mixture is not required.

### Total variation and the behavioral-cloning bound

$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac12\sum_s|p(s)-q(s)|,
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_t^*,d_t^\pi)\le\min\{1,(t-1)\epsilon\},
$$

$$
J_{\mathrm{imit}}(\pi_\theta)
\le\min\left\{H,\epsilon H(H+1)/2\right\}
=O(\epsilon H^2).
$$

## Glossary

- **Action:** the decision or control command produced by a policy.
- **Behavioral cloning:** supervised maximum-likelihood learning from demonstrated observation-action pairs.
- **Cold start:** retraining a model from an earlier initialization rather than continuing from the current fitted weights.
- **DAgger:** Dataset Aggregation, an interactive imitation-learning method that labels states visited by the learned policy.
- **Demonstration trajectory:** a time-ordered sequence of observations and expert actions.
- **Distributional shift:** a mismatch between the distribution used for training and the distribution encountered at evaluation.
- **Fully observed:** the special case in which observation equals state.
- **Horizon:** the number of decision steps in a trajectory.
- **Intervention:** a period in which a human takes control from a learned policy and supplies corrective actions.
- **Markov property:** conditional on the current state and action, earlier states do not add information needed for the next-state distribution.
- **Observation:** information directly available to the agent, such as a camera image.
- **Partially observed:** a setting in which an observation does not reveal the complete state.
- **Policy:** a conditional distribution over actions given an observation or state.
- **Recovery data:** examples that show how to return from an error or perturbation.
- **State:** a representation intended to summarize information relevant to future dynamics and decisions.
- **Total variation divergence:** half the $L_1$ distance between probability distributions.
- **Visitation distribution:** the distribution over states or observations induced by running a policy.
- **Warm start:** continuing training from the current or previously fitted parameters.
