## Part I - The offline setting

## 1. Online, off-policy, and offline RL

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1-424

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Online RL allows the learner to interact with the environment while training. The policy collects new data, updates, and returns to the environment. Off-policy RL can learn from data generated by another policy, but it may still continue collecting new data. Offline RL removes that corrective interaction: the learner receives a fixed dataset and must produce a policy without adding transitions.

The fixed-data setting is attractive when trial and error is dangerous, slow, expensive, or ethically unacceptable. Autonomous-driving logs are plentiful, but deploying an immature exploratory policy can be unsafe. Healthcare data records many decisions and outcomes, while experimenting on patients is constrained and outcomes may take a long time to appear. Human demonstrations and historical logs also contain behavior that one might want to improve without reproducing the data-collection process.

Offline RL is harder precisely because a bad learned policy cannot reveal and correct its mistakes by gathering new experience. The algorithm must reason about actions not fully represented in its dataset while knowing when such reasoning is unreliable.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 2-4 use diagrams to distinguish online, off-policy, and offline data flows. In the offline diagram, the arrow from the learned policy back to the environment is absent.

### Additional explanation

Off-policy and offline are not synonyms. Replay-buffer actor-critic is off policy but still online if its policy continues to add data. Offline RL is defined by the closed dataset, not by a particular learning rule.

## 2. What offline data can provide

**Transcript coverage:** lines 425-1302

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer contrasted data imitation with outcome optimization. AlphaGo is compelling because optimization found behavior beyond human conventions, while a generative model is often judged by how well it reproduces human-like data. Offline RL tries to combine those strengths: learn from an existing dataset but use rewards to select or recombine behavior that is better than the average behavior in it.

Offline training can also initialize later online RL. A large historical dataset may provide representations, value estimates, or successful behaviors before expensive interaction begins.

Three intuitions explain how a fixed dataset could support improvement. First, the algorithm can filter good behavior from bad behavior using rewards. Second, function approximation can generalize from observed state-action pairs to nearby ones. Third, it can "stitch" compatible trajectory pieces. If the data contains behavior from $A$ to $B$ and separate behavior from $B$ to $C$, an RL value function may infer a path from $A$ to $C$ even if no demonstrator executed the complete path.

In the maze illustration, human or exploratory trajectories form a tangled collection of routes. Offline RL aims to infer a direct high-return path by recombining useful segments. In a skill dataset, it may combine the best parts of different people's behavior rather than imitate any one person in full.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 5-9 depict reward-based filtering, generalization, and trajectory stitching. The maze figures visualize the difference between the behavior-data distribution and the desired direct policy.

### Additional explanation

Stitching is primarily a value-propagation idea. Bellman backups can pass the value of reaching $C$ backward through transitions ending at $B$. It succeeds only if the connecting states and relevant actions are represented well enough for the critic to evaluate them reliably.

## 3. Formal objective and counterfactual queries

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1303-1725

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The fixed dataset contains tuples such as $(s_i,a_i,s_i')$ and may also contain rewards. It was generated by an unknown behavior policy $\pi_\beta$. The goal is still to find a policy maximizing expected discounted return in the actual environment, not merely likelihood on the dataset.

A learned transition model or Q-function can be accurate in expectation under the behavior distribution while being wrong under a new policy. Policy improvement poses counterfactual questions: what would happen if an action different from the recorded one were taken? Driving logs may cover on-road actions but not what happens after steering off road. The learner must avoid unsupported out-of-distribution (OOD) decisions without forbidding all useful generalization.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 10-11 state the setting as

$$
\mathcal D=\{(s_i,a_i,r_i,s_i')\}_{i=1}^{N},
\qquad
(s_i,a_i)\sim d^{\pi_\beta},
$$

with objective

$$
\max_\pi J(\pi)
=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi}\!\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)
\right].
$$

The behavior policy need not be known as a normalized density.

### Additional explanation

Offline RL is an identification problem as well as an optimization problem. Two environments can agree on every transition in the dataset yet disagree about an unseen action. Without assumptions such as coverage, smoothness, or conservatism, no algorithm can know which counterfactual is correct.

## Part II - Why ordinary deep RL breaks offline

## 4. Distribution shift and maximization of error

**Transcript coverage:** lines 1726-2637

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

Distribution shift means that the test distribution differs from the training distribution. Merely evaluating new examples from the same distribution is not distribution shift. Empirical risk minimization controls expected error under the training distribution; it says little about errors in regions the training distribution rarely visits.

Optimization makes this especially dangerous. Suppose a learned function is the true function plus prediction error. Taking an argmax over the learned function tends to select points where the error is positive. Even unbiased errors can therefore create a strongly biased optimized result. The lecturer compared this to adversarial examples: the policy searches the Q-function or model's inputs for a mistake that yields a favorable output.

This is not accidental test-time noise. The learned policy is constructed to visit exactly the states and actions where its objective is predicted to be largest, including spurious maxima outside the dataset.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 12-15 show empirical-risk minimization under $p_{\mathrm{train}}$, evaluation under $p_{\mathrm{test}}$, and an argmax landing on a positive approximation error. The diagrams establish that "unbiased error" does not imply an unbiased maximum.

### Additional explanation

If $\hat f(x)=f(x)+\epsilon(x)$, then

$$
\max_x\hat f(x)
$$

selects jointly for large $f(x)$ and large positive $\epsilon(x)$. This optimizer's curse appears in Q-learning, model-based planning, reward-model optimization, and many other learned-objective systems.

## 5. How Q-learning, models, and policy gradients fail offline

**Transcript coverage:** lines 2638-3381

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

In Q-learning, the critic is fitted on behavior-policy actions, but the Bellman target evaluates or maximizes over actions selected by the learned policy. If an OOD action receives an erroneously high Q-value, the maximization selects it and backs that value up to earlier states. The error spreads through the value function like a disease.

Model-based offline RL has the analogous problem. A planner chooses an OOD action because the learned dynamics predict an unrealistically favorable next state. Without new interaction, the model cannot be corrected where the policy exploits it.

Importance-weighted policy gradient offers a formally unbiased route if behavior-policy likelihoods are known and its support covers the learned policy. In long horizons, the product of likelihood ratios has enormous variance as the policy departs from $\pi_\beta$. A KL or reference-policy regularizer can constrain that departure.

An audience member asked how to use $\pi_\beta$ when it is unknown. The lecturer said methods that do not require its density are preferable when possible. Otherwise, one can fit a behavior model, but that introduces another estimation problem.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 16-18 display the offline Bellman target,

$$
y=r+\gamma\mathbb E_{a'\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s')}
[Q_{\bar\phi}(s',a')],
$$

and highlight that $a'$ may be outside the actions represented near $s'$ in $\mathcal D$. The model-based diagram shows the same exploitation through a learned transition model.

### Additional explanation

The dangerous critic query appears in the target before the actor is even deployed. Repeated bootstrapping can amplify one local extrapolation error into very large values throughout predecessor states. Avoiding OOD actions only at final policy execution is therefore too late.

## 6. Empirical Q-value blow-up

**Transcript coverage:** lines 3382-3882

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer showed an offline Soft Actor-Critic experiment using datasets of one thousand, ten thousand, one hundred thousand, and one million transitions. Across these sizes, actual policy return remained around $-250$, while predicted Q-values rose dramatically and could reach approximately $10^{20}$.

The point was not that more data never helps. Rather, a finite dataset can still omit actions the policy discovers through optimization, and bootstrapping repeatedly magnifies their overestimated values. In online RL, executing the policy would generate transitions that disprove those errors. Offline RL has no such automatic correction mechanism.

### Source reconciliation

Slide 19 plots true return against predicted Q-values for several dataset sizes. The $10^{20}$ magnitude is read from the lecturer's account and the plotted scale; it is an example of divergence, not a theoretical upper bound.

### Additional explanation

The experiment demonstrates a calibration failure: critic loss on the fixed dataset can look reasonable while the critic is catastrophically wrong on policy-selected actions. Offline evaluation must therefore examine policy support and predicted-versus-real outcomes where possible, not only training loss.

## Part III - Controlling distribution shift

## 7. Three offline-RL principles

**Transcript coverage:** lines 3883-4293

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

The lecturer organized offline-RL remedies around three principles.

1. **Policy constraints:** keep the learned policy sufficiently close to the behavior data so its Q-function or model is queried in reliable regions.
2. **Pessimism:** assign low value to unsupported actions or outcomes, so optimization does not select them.
3. **Avoid OOD queries:** design the learning target so that the critic is never asked to evaluate an unsupported action in the first place.

For policy constraints, optimize expected Q subject to, or regularized by, a divergence from the behavior policy. The remainder of the lecture began comparing forward and reverse KL as two such divergences.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 20-21 list the three principles and give the generic regularized actor objective

$$
\max_\theta
\mathbb E_{s\sim\mathcal D,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}
[Q_\phi(s,a)]
-\lambda D(\pi_\theta,\pi_\beta).
$$

### Additional explanation

These principles can overlap. A support-constrained policy prevents OOD targets; a pessimistic critic makes OOD actions unattractive; an implicit algorithm may construct targets solely from dataset actions. Many named offline-RL algorithms combine more than one.

## 8. Forward and reverse KL policy constraints

**Transcript coverage:** lines 4294-4792

### What the lecturer said - transcript only

A policy constraint expresses the belief that the learned value function is most reliable near the behavior data. It should still permit improvement, so the question is how to define "near."

Forward KL,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\beta\,\|\,\pi_\theta),
$$

averages under the behavior policy. With a restricted unimodal policy fitted to multimodal data, it tends to cover the modes or moment-match them, potentially placing probability between modes.

Reverse KL,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_\beta),
$$

averages under the learned policy. It strongly penalizes selecting an action to which $\pi_\beta$ assigns zero probability and tends to select one supported mode rather than cover all modes. This is appealing when the objective is to choose the best behavior present in the data, but it requires evaluating or estimating $\log\pi_\beta(a\mid s)$.

The audience leaned toward reverse KL for offline control because mode seeking avoids probability between demonstrated behaviors. The lecturer contrasted this with simple imitation and with reward hacking, then the recording ends mid-sentence before he completes the comparison.

### Source reconciliation

Slides 22-23 contain the forward/reverse KL equations and mode-covering/mode-seeking diagrams. The transcript's automatic "Kale" renderings are reconciled to KL using those slides. The missing continuation is not reconstructed here.

### Additional explanation

Direction matters because KL is asymmetric. Forward KL is easy to estimate with behavior samples:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\beta\|\pi_\theta)
=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\beta}
[\log\pi_\beta(a\mid s)-\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)].
$$

Ignoring the constant behavior entropy, minimizing it is behavioral cloning. Reverse KL requires samples from $\pi_\theta$ and behavior density values at those samples, which is precisely where behavior-model errors can matter.

## Consolidated takeaways

1. Offline RL learns from a fixed dataset and cannot use new interaction to correct policy-induced errors.
2. Reward-based filtering, function approximation, and Bellman stitching can improve over average behavior in the dataset.
3. Improvement requires counterfactual predictions, but unsupported counterfactuals are not identifiable without assumptions.
4. An optimizer selects positive model or critic errors, so ordinary generalization error becomes a policy-exploitation problem.
5. Offline Q-learning can bootstrap one OOD overestimate into enormous upstream values.
6. Policy constraints, pessimism, and target designs that avoid OOD queries are the three central remedy families.
7. Forward KL is behavior-sample friendly and mode covering; reverse KL is mode seeking but requires a behavior density model.
8. An ideal support constraint would allow improvement anywhere the behavior policy has support and penalize only unsupported actions, but support is hard to estimate.

## Key equations

### Offline policy objective

$$
J(\pi)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi}
\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)\right].
$$

### Offline Bellman target

$$
y=r+\gamma\mathbb E_{a'\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s')}
[Q_{\bar\phi}(s',a')].
$$

### Policy-constrained actor objective

$$
\max_\theta\;
\mathbb E_{s\sim\mathcal D,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}[Q_\phi(s,a)]
-\lambda D(\pi_\theta,\pi_\beta).
$$

### Forward KL

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\beta\|\pi_\theta)
=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\beta}
\left[\log\frac{\pi_\beta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}\right].
$$

### Reverse KL

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_\beta)
=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}
\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\beta(a\mid s)}\right].
$$

## Glossary

- **Online RL:** RL in which the current learner continues interacting with the environment.
- **Off-policy RL:** Learning about one policy from data generated by another policy.
- **Offline RL:** Learning a policy from a fixed dataset without further environment interaction during training.
- **Behavior policy $\pi_\beta$:** The unknown or known policy distribution that generated the offline data.
- **Trajectory stitching:** Combining valuable transition segments from different recorded trajectories through value propagation.
- **Counterfactual query:** Asking a learned model or critic about an action different from the one recorded in the dataset.
- **Out of distribution (OOD):** Lying in a region poorly represented by the training distribution.
- **Optimizer's curse:** Selection of an option partly because its estimate contains a favorable error.
- **Bootstrapping:** Training a value estimate from another value estimate, allowing errors to propagate through time.
- **Policy constraint:** A divergence or support restriction that keeps learned actions near behavior data.
- **Pessimism:** Deliberately lowering estimates for uncertain or unsupported actions.
- **Forward KL:** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\beta\|\pi_\theta)$, often associated with behavior cloning and mode coverage.
- **Reverse KL:** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_\beta)$, often associated with mode seeking and explicit behavior-density evaluation.
- **Support constraint:** A restriction penalizing actions outside the set assigned nonzero behavior probability.
